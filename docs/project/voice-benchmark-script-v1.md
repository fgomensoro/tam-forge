# Voice benchmark reading script v1

**Version:** `voice-benchmark-script-v1`. **Purpose:** the reference transcript for issue #43. Read it aloud exactly as written; the scorer compares each model's transcript against this text, so no manual transcription is needed.

**Privacy:** this script contains no employer, customer, participant, or personal data. Every company and person named here is invented. Recordings of it stay in `apps/macos/PrivateAudio/`, which is never committed.

**How to record.** Open QuickTime Player, choose File then New Audio Recording, pick the built-in microphone, and record each passage as its own file in a quiet room at a normal speaking distance. Save them as `passage-1.m4a` through `passage-6.m4a` in `apps/macos/PrivateAudio/`. Read at your natural pace and accent; do not imitate a newsreader. If you stumble, keep going rather than restarting, because passage 5 is meant to contain exactly that kind of repair.

**Critical terms** (the scorer tracks these separately because losing them changes meaning): total addressable market, serviceable addressable market, bottom-up, top-down, segmentation, penetration, churn, cohort, gross margin, unit economics, pipeline, quota, discovery call, stakeholder, procurement, renewal, expansion revenue, forecast, attach rate, win rate.

---

## Passage 1 — steady technical explanation

The total addressable market is the full revenue opportunity available if every buyer who could use the product actually bought it. The serviceable addressable market narrows that to the segment we can reach with the channels and the pricing we have today. I prefer a bottom-up estimate over a top-down one, because a bottom-up model forces me to name the number of accounts, the average contract value, and the attach rate, and each of those can be checked against something real. A top-down number sounds confident and hides its assumptions.

## Passage 2 — numbers and units

We modelled four thousand two hundred accounts in the mid market and eleven hundred in the enterprise segment. Average contract value was fourteen thousand dollars a year in the mid market and ninety one thousand in enterprise. At a twelve percent penetration rate over three years, that gives roughly seven million dollars of annual recurring revenue, with a gross margin near seventy eight percent. Churn was six percent annually in the mid market cohort and two percent in enterprise. The unit economics only work if the payback period stays under fourteen months.

## Passage 3 — fast delivery

Right, so the pipeline for this quarter looks thin at the top and heavy at the bottom, which usually means we are going to hit the number this quarter and miss it next quarter, and the fix is not more discovery calls, the fix is better qualification on the calls we already have, because the win rate on unqualified opportunities is under nine percent and every one of them still costs a full demo, a security review, and two weeks of somebody's attention.

## Passage 4 — deliberate pauses

The first question is who the buyer actually is.

[Pause for three seconds.]

In most of these deals the economic buyer sits in finance, the technical stakeholder sits in engineering, and procurement enters late and slows everything down.

[Pause for three seconds.]

So the forecast has to account for a procurement cycle that nobody controls, and the renewal conversation has to start long before the renewal date.

## Passage 5 — restarts and self-corrections

The expansion revenue last year was, sorry, let me start that again. Expansion revenue last year was about thirty percent of new bookings. No, that is not right either, it was closer to thirty five percent if you include seat growth but only twenty two percent if you count new modules alone. The reason I keep correcting myself is that we report it two different ways, and the two ways disagree by more than ten points, which is exactly the kind of thing a stakeholder notices in the first five minutes.

## Passage 6 — quiet speech and mixed vocabulary

Speaking more quietly now, because the last part of an interview is often the part where somebody drops their voice. Segmentation is the piece people skip. If the segments are wrong then the cohort analysis is wrong, the quota model is wrong, and the forecast inherits both errors. I would rather present a smaller market with a defensible segmentation than a very large number that falls apart under a single question about the denominator.
